How to breathe while running

By the RunZone team · Updated November 2026

Breathe through your mouth and nose together, from your belly rather than your chest, and let the rhythm follow your effort instead of forcing it. If you can't speak a full sentence on an easy run, the problem usually isn't your breathing technique: you're running too fast, so slow down until you can talk.

Mouth, nose or both?

Use both. At an easy pace you may be able to breathe mostly through your nose, and some runners use that as a check that they're keeping it easy. As effort rises your body needs more air than your nose can move, so mouth breathing takes over. That's normal, not a sign you're doing it wrong. Forcing nose-only breathing on hard efforts just makes them feel harder.

Breathe from your belly

Shallow chest breathing uses only the top of your lungs and tends to feel panicky. Belly (diaphragmatic) breathing fills them more fully. Practise it off the run first:

  1. Lie on your back with one hand on your chest and one on your stomach.
  2. Breathe in through your nose so the hand on your stomach rises and the one on your chest barely moves.
  3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth, letting your stomach fall.
  4. Do 5 minutes a day for a week, then try it standing, then on a slow walk.
  5. On your next easy run, check in every few minutes: shoulders down, stomach moving.

Match your breathing rhythm to your effort

Many runners find a steady pattern of steps per breath calming. Count footsteps: "in for 3 steps, out for 3" is written 3:3. You don't have to use a pattern, but it's a useful guide to how hard you're working.

EffortTypical patternTalk test
Easy / Zone 23:3 or 3:2Full sentences
Steady / Zone 32:2Short phrases
Hard intervals2:1 or 1:1A word or two

Some coaches suggest odd patterns like 3:2 so your exhale lands on alternate feet, which may spread the load more evenly. There's no strong evidence it matters, so use whatever feels natural. Why you feel out of breath on easy runs

Most new runners start too fast. Running feels like it should be quick, so the first few minutes go out at a pace you can't hold, your breathing races, and you blame your lungs. Try these instead:

  • Slow down more than feels right. If you're gasping, walk for a minute, then restart slower. Walk breaks are normal early on.
  • Warm up. Five minutes of brisk walking lets your breathing rise gradually instead of jumping.
  • Exhale fully. Breathlessness often comes from not emptying your lungs. A long, relaxed out-breath makes the next in-breath easier.
  • Relax your upper body. Drop your shoulders and unclench your hands and jaw.

Side stitches

A side stitch is a sharp pain under the ribs, common in new runners. Its cause isn't fully understood. If one hits: slow to a walk, breathe out slowly and deeply, and press gently into the painful spot. Avoiding a big meal in the two hours before a run and building speed gradually help many people.

Breathing in cold air

Cold, dry air can make your throat and chest feel tight. A buff or neck gaiter pulled loosely over your mouth warms the air a little, and keeping the pace easy keeps your breathing calmer. See what to wear running in the cold for the rest of your kit.

This is general fitness information, not medical advice. If you get chest pain, wheezing, dizziness, or breathlessness that doesn't settle when you slow down or stop, or that is getting worse over time, stop running and see a doctor.

FAQ

Should you breathe through your nose or mouth when running?

Both. Nose breathing works at easy efforts, but as you run harder you need your mouth too. Breathing through both together is normal and lets you move the most air.

How do I stop getting out of breath when running?

Slow down until you can speak in full sentences, take walk breaks if needed, warm up with a brisk walk, and focus on long, relaxed exhales from your belly. Most breathlessness in new runners comes from starting too fast.

What is the 3:2 breathing pattern?

Breathing in for three footsteps and out for two. It's a common rhythm for easy running. Some coaches like it because the exhale alternates feet, but any comfortable, steady rhythm is fine.
